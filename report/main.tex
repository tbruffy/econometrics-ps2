\section*{Problem 1(a)}

Define $lwage_i=\log(wage_i)$. For each education level
$e\in\{12,\ldots,17\}$ and age $a\in\{26,\ldots,30\}$,
define
\[
d_{e,a,i}
=\mathbf{1}\{education_i=e,\ age_i=a\}.
\]

We estimate the regression
\[
lwage_i
=\sum_{e=12}^{17}\sum_{a=26}^{30}
\alpha_{e,a}d_{e,a,i}+\epsilon_i.
\]
No additional intercept is included because the
30 dummy variables sum to one for every observation.

Since $E(\epsilon_i\mid education_i,age_i)=0$,
\[
\alpha_{e,a}
=f(e,a)
=E[lwage_i\mid education_i=e,\ age_i=a].
\]
Each coefficient is therefore the population mean
log wage for its education--age group. Its OLS
estimate is the corresponding sample mean log wage.
This specification permits a separate conditional
mean for every group.

Table~\ref{tab:ps1a} reports the estimates and
conventional standard errors, which assume a
common error variance across groups.

\section*{Problem 1(b)}

We test whether the unrestricted conditional means satisfy
\[
H_0:\quad
\alpha_{e,a}
=\beta_0+\beta_1 e+\beta_2 a+\beta_3 ea
\]
for all education--age combinations, against the
alternative that no such coefficient vector exists.

The unrestricted model has 30 parameters, whereas
the restricted model has four. The null therefore
imposes 26 independent restrictions.

\subsection*{Specification of $R$ and $q$}

Choose the four corner groups
\[
\mathcal{C}
=\{(12,26),(12,30),(17,26),(17,30)\}.
\]
For each of the remaining 26 groups $(e,a)$, define
\[
u=\frac{e-12}{5},
\qquad
v=\frac{a-26}{4}.
\]
Under the null hypothesis, the following restriction holds:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\alpha_{e,a}
&-(1-u)(1-v)\alpha_{12,26}\\
&-(1-u)v\alpha_{12,30}\\
&-u(1-v)\alpha_{17,26}
-uv\alpha_{17,30}=0.
\end{aligned}
\]

These equations specify $H_0:R\alpha=q$, where
$R$ is a $26\times30$ matrix and
$q=\mathbf{0}_{26}$.

The columns of $R$ follow the ordering of $\alpha$
given in the question. For each non-corner group,
the corresponding row has a coefficient of one
on $\alpha_{e,a}$, the four negative weights above
on the corner coefficients, and zeros elsewhere.
Order the rows by education and then age,
omitting the four corner groups.

For example, the restriction for $(e,a)=(12,27)$ is
\[
\alpha_{12,27}
-\frac34\alpha_{12,26}
-\frac14\alpha_{12,30}=0.
\]

The restrictions are independent because each
non-corner coefficient appears in exactly one row
with coefficient one. Thus $\operatorname{rank}(R)=26$.

\subsection*{F-test}

The estimated restricted conditional mean is
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{E}[lwage\mid education,age]
={}&6.107505+0.197808\,education\\
&+0.064883\,age\\
&-0.002520(education\times age).
\end{aligned}
\]

The residual sums of squares are
\[
SSR_U=608.0274,
\qquad
SSR_R=624.7819.
\]
With $n=898$, the conventional F-statistic is
\[
F
=\frac{(SSR_R-SSR_U)/26}{SSR_U/(898-30)}
=\frac{(624.7819-608.0274)/26}{608.0274/868}
\approx0.9199.
\]

Using the $F_{26,868}$ reference distribution gives
a p-value of approximately $0.5806$.

Since $0.5806>0.05$, we do not reject the null
hypothesis at the 5\% significance level.
The restrictions do not produce a statistically
significant deterioration in fit under the
conventional F-test assumptions. This does not
establish that the restricted model is true.

\section*{Problem 2(a)}

Consider the regression
\[
y_i=\beta x_i+\epsilon_i,
\qquad
x_i=
\begin{cases}
i, & \text{if } i \text{ is even},\\
0, & \text{if } i \text{ is odd},
\end{cases}
\]
where the errors are independent and identically
distributed as $N(0,1)$.

We show that $\widehat{\beta}\xrightarrow{p}\beta$.

For $n\geq2$, the OLS estimator in this regression
without an intercept is
\[
\widehat{\beta}
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i y_i}
       {\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.
\]
Substituting $y_i=\beta x_i+\epsilon_i$ gives
\[
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\epsilon_i}
       {\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.
\]

The regressors are deterministic, and
$E(\epsilon_i)=0$. Therefore,
\[
E(\widehat{\beta}-\beta)=0.
\]
Since the errors are independent and have
variance one,
\[
\operatorname{Var}\left(
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\epsilon_i
\right)
=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta})
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}
       {\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2\right)^2}
=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.
\]

There are $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ even indices,
each contributing at least four to the sum
of squared regressors. Hence,
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2
=4\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}j^2
\geq4\left\lfloor\frac n2\right\rfloor
\longrightarrow\infty.
\]
It follows that
$\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta})\to0$.

Finally, by Chebyshev's inequality, for every
$\delta>0$,
\[
P\left(
|\widehat{\beta}-\beta|>\delta
\right)
\leq
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta})}{\delta^2}
=
\frac{1}{\delta^2\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}
\longrightarrow0.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{\widehat{\beta}\xrightarrow{p}\beta,}
\]
so the OLS estimator is consistent.
\section*{Problem 2(b)}

Now suppose $x_i=\lambda^i$, where $|\lambda|<1$.
First consider $0<|\lambda|<1$.

As in part (a), the OLS estimation error is
\[
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda^i\epsilon_i}
       {\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda^{2i}}.
\]
Define
\[
S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda^{2i}.
\]
Since the errors are independent with mean zero
and variance one,
\[
E(\widehat{\beta})=\beta,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta})=\frac{1}{S_n}.
\]

Using the geometric-series formula,
\[
S_n
=\frac{\lambda^2(1-\lambda^{2n})}{1-\lambda^2}
\longrightarrow
\frac{\lambda^2}{1-\lambda^2}<\infty.
\]
Hence,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta})
\longrightarrow
\frac{1-\lambda^2}{\lambda^2}>0.
\]

A positive limiting variance alone does not
establish inconsistency. However, because the
estimation error is a linear combination of
independent normal errors,
\[
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
\sim N\left(0,\frac{1}{S_n}\right).
\]
Therefore, for any fixed $\delta>0$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
P\left(|\widehat{\beta}-\beta|>\delta\right)
&=2\left[1-\Phi\left(\delta\sqrt{S_n}\right)\right]\\
&\longrightarrow
2\left[
1-\Phi\left(
\frac{\delta|\lambda|}{\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}}
\right)
\right]
>0,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative
distribution function.

Thus the probability of a fixed estimation error
does not converge to zero, so
\[
\boxed{
\widehat{\beta}\text{ is inconsistent for }\beta
\text{ when }0<|\lambda|<1.
}
\]

Although the estimator is unbiased, later
observations contribute progressively less
information because the regressors shrink
geometrically towards zero.

If $\lambda=0$, then $x_i=0$ for every observation.
In this case, $\beta$ is unidentified and the
OLS coefficient is undefined.
\section*{Problem 2(c)}

The Gauss--Markov assumptions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(GM1)] Full column rank:
    $\operatorname{rank}(X)=k$.
    \item[(GM2)] Strict exogeneity:
    $E(\epsilon\mid X)=0$.
    \item[(GM3)] Homoskedasticity and no serial
    correlation:
    $\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I_n$.
\end{enumerate}

There is one regressor and no intercept, so
$k=1$. Full column rank therefore requires
\[
X'X=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2>0.
\]

For the regressor sequence in part (a), GM1 holds
for $n\geq2$, since $x_2=2\neq0$.
The zero entries at odd indices do not prevent
the single regressor column from having full rank.

For the sequence $x_i=\lambda^i$ in part (b),
GM1 holds whenever $\lambda\neq0$, since
$x_1=\lambda\neq0$.

In both cases, $X$ is deterministic.
Consequently, conditioning on $X$ does not change
the distribution of the errors. Since the errors
are i.i.d.\ $N(0,1)$,
\[
E(\epsilon\mid X)=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon\mid X)=I_n.
\]
Thus GM2 and GM3 hold, with $\sigma^2=1$.

Therefore, all three Gauss--Markov assumptions
hold in part (a) for $n\geq2$, and in part (b)
for $0<|\lambda|<1$. OLS is consequently the
best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE) in both cases.
If $\lambda=0$, GM1 fails and the OLS coefficient
is undefined.

There is no contradiction between this result
and the inconsistency established in part (b).
The Gauss--Markov theorem is a finite-sample
variance comparison among linear unbiased
estimators. It does not guarantee that the
variance of OLS tends to zero as $n$ increases.

In this problem,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{\beta}\mid X)
=\frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}.
\]
This variance tends to zero in part (a), but
approaches a strictly positive constant in
part (b). Hence OLS can be BLUE without
being consistent.
\section*{Problem 2(d)}

Suppose $x_i=\lambda^i$, with $0<|\lambda|<1$.
As established in part (b),
\[
\widehat{\beta}-\beta
=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda^i\epsilon_i}{S_n},
\qquad
S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda^{2i}.
\]

Because the errors are independent normal
variables, the estimator has the exact
finite-sample distribution
\[
\widehat{\beta}
\sim N\left(\beta,\frac{1}{S_n}\right).
\]

The geometric-series formula gives
\[
S_n
=\frac{\lambda^2(1-\lambda^{2n})}{1-\lambda^2}
\longrightarrow
\frac{\lambda^2}{1-\lambda^2}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\frac{1}{S_n}
\longrightarrow
\frac{1-\lambda^2}{\lambda^2}>0.
\]

Since the normal means remain equal to $\beta$
and the variances converge, it follows that
\[
\boxed{
\widehat{\beta}\xrightarrow{d}
N\left(
\beta,\frac{1-\lambda^2}{\lambda^2}
\right).
}
\]

The limiting distribution is nondegenerate:
its variance is strictly positive. This is
consistent with the inconsistency shown in
part (b).

If $\lambda=0$, the OLS coefficient is undefined,
so the result applies only to $0<|\lambda|<1$.

\section*{Problem 3(a)}

Under the null hypothesis,
$\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=\sigma^2>0$.
We show that
\[
T=
\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}}
\left(\log s_1^2-\log s_2^2\right)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2).
\]

First consider either sample $j\in\{1,2\}$.
Normality implies
\[
Q_j=\frac{(n_j-1)s_j^2}{\sigma^2}
\sim\chi^2_{n_j-1}.
\]
Write $m_j=n_j-1$. A chi-squared variable with
$m_j$ degrees of freedom has the distribution
of a sum of $m_j$ independent squared standard
normal variables.

For $Z\sim N(0,1)$,
\[
E(Z^2)=1,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Z^2)
=E(Z^4)-[E(Z^2)]^2
=3-1=2.
\]
The central limit theorem therefore gives
\[
\frac{Q_j-m_j}{\sqrt{m_j}}
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2).
\]
Since
\[
\frac{Q_j-m_j}{\sqrt{m_j}}
=
\frac{\sqrt{m_j}}{\sigma^2}
(s_j^2-\sigma^2),
\]
and $\sqrt{n_j/m_j}\to1$, it follows that
\[
\sqrt{n_j}(s_j^2-\sigma^2)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2\sigma^4).
\]

Now apply the delta method with $g(x)=\log x$.
Because $g'(\sigma^2)=1/\sigma^2$,
\[
A_j
:=\sqrt{n_j}
\left(\log s_j^2-\log\sigma^2\right)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2).
\]
The asymptotic variance is
$(1/\sigma^2)^2(2\sigma^4)=2$.

The samples are independent, so the joint limit is
\[
(A_1,A_2)\xrightarrow{d}(Z_1,Z_2),
\]
where $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are independent
$N(0,2)$ random variables.

Under the null, the common population
log variance cancels, giving
\[
T=
\sqrt{\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}}\,A_1
-
\sqrt{\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}}\,A_2.
\]
Since $n_1/n_2\to c\in(0,\infty)$,
\[
\sqrt{\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}}
\longrightarrow\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c}},
\qquad
\sqrt{\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}}
\longrightarrow\sqrt{\frac{c}{1+c}}.
\]
By Slutsky's theorem,
\[
T\xrightarrow{d}
\frac{Z_1}{\sqrt{1+c}}
-\sqrt{\frac{c}{1+c}}\,Z_2.
\]

This limit is normal with mean zero.
Independence implies that its variance is
\[
\frac{2}{1+c}+\frac{2c}{1+c}=2.
\]
Therefore,
\[
\boxed{T\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2).}
\]
\section*{Problem 3(b)}

We work under normality and the null hypothesis
$\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2$. All limits are taken as
$n_1,n_2\to\infty$ with $n_1/n_2\to c\in(0,\infty)$.

Define
\[
V_n=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2},
\qquad
k_n=\frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2},
\qquad
T_n=\sqrt{k_n}\log V_n.
\]
Under these assumptions,
\[
V_n\sim F_{n_1-1,n_2-1}.
\]

Let $f_n$ denote the 95th percentile of this
F-distribution. The finite-sample test rejects
when $V_n>f_n$. Since the logarithm is strictly
increasing, this is equivalent to
\[
T_n>b_n,
\qquad
b_n=\sqrt{k_n}\log f_n.
\]
The asymptotic test rejects when
\[
T_n>a,
\qquad
a=\sqrt{2}\,z_{0.95},
\]
where $z_{0.95}=\Phi^{-1}(0.95)$.

We first show that $b_n\to a$.
The monotonic transformation implies that
$b_n$ is the exact 95th percentile of $T_n$
under the normal null hypothesis.

Let $H_n$ be the cumulative distribution
function of $T_n$. By part (a),
\[
T_n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,2),
\]
whose cumulative distribution function is
\[
H(t)=\Phi(t/\sqrt{2}).
\]
This function is continuous and strictly
increasing, with $H(a)=0.95$.

For any $\varepsilon>0$,
\[
H(a-\varepsilon)<0.95<H(a+\varepsilon).
\]
Convergence in distribution implies that,
for sufficiently large sample sizes,
\[
H_n(a-\varepsilon)<0.95<H_n(a+\varepsilon).
\]
Therefore,
\[
a-\varepsilon<b_n<a+\varepsilon,
\]
which establishes $b_n\to a$.

The two tests can disagree only when $T_n$
lies between $a$ and $b_n$. For any
$\varepsilon>0$, eventually
$|b_n-a|<\varepsilon$, so
\[
P(\text{disagreement})
\leq
P(a-\varepsilon\leq T_n\leq a+\varepsilon).
\]
Since the limiting distribution is continuous,
\[
\limsup P(\text{disagreement})
\leq
H(a+\varepsilon)-H(a-\varepsilon).
\]
Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and using
continuity of $H$ gives
\[
\boxed{
P(\text{the two tests disagree})\longrightarrow0.
}
\]

Thus the finite-sample normal-theory test and
the asymptotic test are asymptotically
equivalent under the null hypothesis.
\section*{Problem 3(c)}

Suppose the two populations have the common
standardized distribution $F_0$. Write
\[
Y_{ji}=\mu_j+\sigma_j Z_{ji},
\]
where the standardized observations satisfy
\[
E(Z_{ji})=0,
\qquad
E(Z_{ji}^2)=1,
\qquad
E(Z_{ji}^4)=\kappa<\infty.
\]
The observations are i.i.d.\ within each sample,
and the two samples are independent.
Here $\kappa$ denotes ordinary kurtosis,
not excess kurtosis.

We first note that
\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_{ji}^2)
=E(Z_{ji}^4)-[E(Z_{ji}^2)]^2
=\kappa-1.
\]
The central limit theorem therefore gives
\[
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_j}}
\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}(Z_{ji}^2-1)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\kappa-1).
\]

Let $\bar Z_j$ denote the sample mean of the
standardized observations. The sample variance
satisfies
\[
\frac{s_j^2}{\sigma_j^2}
=
\frac{n_j}{n_j-1}
\left[
\frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}Z_{ji}^2
-\bar Z_j^2
\right].
\]

Since $\bar Z_j=O_p(n_j^{-1/2})$,
\[
\sqrt{n_j}\,\bar Z_j^2
=O_p(n_j^{-1/2})
=o_p(1).
\]
Also, $n_j/(n_j-1)=1+O(n_j^{-1})$, so the
degrees-of-freedom correction has a negligible
effect at the $\sqrt{n_j}$ scale.
Consequently,
\[
\sqrt{n_j}
\left(\frac{s_j^2}{\sigma_j^2}-1\right)
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_j}}
\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}(Z_{ji}^2-1)
+o_p(1).
\]
By Slutsky's theorem,
\[
\sqrt{n_j}
\left(\frac{s_j^2}{\sigma_j^2}-1\right)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\kappa-1).
\]

Now apply the delta method with $g(x)=\log x$
around $x=1$. Since $g'(1)=1$,
\[
A_j
:=
\sqrt{n_j}
\left(\log s_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2\right)
\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\kappa-1).
\]
Independence of the samples gives the joint limit
\[
(A_1,A_2)\xrightarrow{d}(W_1,W_2),
\]
where $W_1$ and $W_2$ are independent
$N(0,\kappa-1)$ random variables.

Under the null hypothesis,
$\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2$, the population log
variances cancel. Therefore,
\[
T=
\sqrt{\frac{n_2}{n_1+n_2}}\,A_1
-
\sqrt{\frac{n_1}{n_1+n_2}}\,A_2.
\]
As $n_1,n_2\to\infty$ with
$n_1/n_2\to c\in(0,\infty)$, Slutsky's theorem
implies
\[
T\xrightarrow{d}
\frac{W_1}{\sqrt{1+c}}
-\sqrt{\frac{c}{1+c}}\,W_2.
\]

This limit is normal with mean zero.
Its variance is
\[
\frac{\kappa-1}{1+c}
+\frac{c(\kappa-1)}{1+c}
=\kappa-1.
\]
Hence,
\[
\boxed{T\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\kappa-1).}
\]

For a normal population, $\kappa=3$, which
recovers the $N(0,2)$ limit obtained in part (a).
\section*{Problem 3(d)}

The normal-theory test uses the $N(0,2)$
reference distribution. Its asymptotic
critical value is
\[
a=\sqrt{2}\,z_{0.95}
\approx\sqrt{2}(1.644854)
\approx2.326174.
\]
As established in part (b), the transformed
finite-sample F-test cutoff also converges
to this value.

However, when the true population kurtosis
is $\kappa=5$, part (c) gives
\[
T\xrightarrow{d}N(0,\kappa-1)=N(0,4).
\]
Since this limiting distribution is continuous,
the actual asymptotic rejection probability
under the null is
\[
\begin{aligned}
P\bigl(N(0,4)>a\bigr)
&=1-\Phi\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)\\
&=1-\Phi\left(\frac{z_{0.95}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
&\approx1-\Phi(1.163087)\\
&\approx0.1224.
\end{aligned}
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{
\text{Actual asymptotic size}\approx12.24\%.
}
\]

The test is oversized: although its nominal
significance level is $5\%$, its asymptotic
probability of rejecting a true null is
approximately $12.24\%$.

The higher kurtosis increases the asymptotic
variance of $T$, making the normal-theory
critical value too low.
\section*{Problem 3(e)}

Let group 1 denote females and group 2 denote
males, and define $Y_{ji}$ as log hourly wage.
We test
\[
H_0:\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2
\qquad\text{against}\qquad
H_1:\sigma_1^2>\sigma_2^2.
\]
The sample contains $n_1=1569$ females and
$n_2=1727$ males. The sample log-wage variances are
\[
s_1^2=0.397877,
\qquad
s_2^2=0.366413.
\]

The log-variance statistic is
\[
T=
\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}}
\left(\log s_1^2-\log s_2^2\right)
=2.362096.
\]

Using deviations from each group's own sample
mean, we estimate kurtosis by
\[
\widehat{\kappa}
=
\frac{
(n_1+n_2)
\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}
(Y_{ji}-\bar Y_j)^4
}{
\left[
\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}
(Y_{ji}-\bar Y_j)^2
\right]^2
}
=7.378121.
\]

Under the framework in part (c), the
kurtosis-adjusted statistic is
\[
Z_{\mathrm{adj}}
=\frac{T}{\sqrt{\widehat{\kappa}-1}}
=0.935300.
\]
Comparing this statistic with $N(0,1)$ gives
the one-sided p-value
\[
p_{\mathrm{adj}}
=1-\Phi(0.935300)
\approx0.174817.
\]
Since this exceeds $0.05$, we do not reject
equal population log-wage variances at the
5\% significance level.

For comparison, the standard normal-theory
test uses
\[
F=\frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}=1.085871.
\]
Under normality and the null hypothesis,
this statistic follows an $F_{1568,1726}$
distribution. Its upper-tail p-value is
approximately $0.047347$, so the conventional
test rejects at the 5\% level.

decisions. Estimated kurtosis is substantially
above the normal-distribution value of three.
The kurtosis adjustment accounts for the
greater asymptotic variability of the
log-variance statistic.

Although the female sample variance is higher,
the adjusted test does not provide statistically
significant evidence that the female population
log-wage variance exceeds the male variance.
The conventional rejection is sensitive to
the normality assumption.
